What really drives employee performance, and who needs support

An analysis of 1,200 employees across six departments. It finds where performance differs, which three factors matter most, and how a small, well defined group can be identified so that action stays narrow and morale stays protected.

01

Department wise performance

Differences between departments are statistically significant. The lowest rating observed in the data is Good, so the share rated Good is read as the share of weakest performers.

Share rated Good

Lower is better. The marker shows the company average of 16.2 percent.

Average rating

Bars start at 2 (Good) and end at 3.2, to make small gaps visible.
Departments are not unhappier than each other. Between 37 and 43 percent of employees in the five larger departments report a low environment score. What differs is what a low score leads to: of those employees, 79 percent in Finance and 54 percent in Sales are rated Good, against only 9 percent in Development. Development is the first place to look for practices worth copying. Data Science has 20 employees, so no conclusion is drawn for it.

The three most important factors

Confirmed by three independent checks: shuffling each feature on unseen employees, the model's own importance, and retraining without the feature.

1Environment satisfaction. A score of 1 or 2 means about a 40 percent chance of the Good rating; 3 or 4 means about 1 percent.
2Size of the last salary hike. It separates Outstanding (median 21 percent) from the rest (14 percent). It is probably set together with the rating, so it is a companion, not a lever.
3Years since the last promotion. With a promotion in the last year hardly anyone in the low score group is rated Good.

An honest note on hiring. A model built only from candidate information (education, experience, role) did no better than always predicting Excellent. The strongest drivers exist only after a person joins, so the model is recommended for the current workforce, for new hires after their first appraisal, and for what if planning, not for screening candidates.

On the held back employees, the 15 percent with the highest predicted risk (36 people) contained 33 of the 39 who were rated Good, with a precision of 92 percent. A department wide penalty in Sales would reach 77 percent of people who are not rated Good.
05

Recommendations

Each one follows from the evidence above. The data shows association, so results should be tracked after any change.

  1. Act on the working environment firstAbout 39 percent of staff score it 1 or 2. Run a short diagnostic to find the causes, beginning with Sales where the share is highest.
  2. Review the promotion cycle for the low score groupOffer a structured career conversation within the year and review how promotion decisions are made.
  3. Support before penaltyOffer coaching, workload review and development plans to the small identified group, and re-score every quarter.
  4. Learn from Development and check rating consistencyFind out whether its low rate of Good ratings reflects better support, different work or a different appraisal standard, and calibrate appraisals across departments.
  5. Keep reward decisions transparentExplain hike and rating decisions consistently. Higher hikes alone are not a proven way to raise ratings.
  6. Use the model in the right placeCurrent workforce, new hires after the first appraisal, and planning, always with human judgement. Start collecting pre hire assessments if a true hiring model is wanted.
  7. Improve the dataRecord ratings over several periods, manager and team, workload, reasons behind low scores and client satisfaction by team.
  8. Keep monitoring fairnessGender, marital status and age played no useful part. Check periodically that predictions and actions do not differ across groups.
